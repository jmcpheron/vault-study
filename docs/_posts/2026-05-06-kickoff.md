# Why we're rebuilding Adam Savage's vault door in CAD

We watched Adam Savage machine a 1/12-scale bank-vault door on his
Tested YouTube series and couldn't stop thinking about the mechanism:
one 120-tooth ring gear, twelve 24-tooth spur gears, twelve locking
pins, all timed off tooth counts you can verify by pausing the video.
So we're rebuilding it in Onshape, in public.

The pull is that this mechanism is *legible*. Most lock mechanisms
hide their cleverness; this one wears it. Turn the ring gear and
twelve rack-and-pinions shoot twelve pins radially outward in
perfect unison — and the reason they stay in unison is arithmetic,
not craftsmanship: 120 divides by 12, so every spur gear sees the
same ten teeth of ring gear between neighbours. Adam has to *machine*
that identically twelve times. We get it for free with a circular
pattern, which feels like cheating and is exactly why it's a good
first assemblies project.

What we want to learn, concretely: Onshape assemblies end-to-end —
gear relations, rack-and-pinion mates, sub-assembly patterns, and
the discipline of driving a whole model from a variable sheet.

What "done" looks like, in two acts:

1. **A public Onshape document anyone can open** and drag the
   mechanism around in — [it's live now ↗](https://cad.onshape.com/documents/eaf3e87c1faae12ad867b335/w/83a96bb8921d1af3abd7aecd/e/9db299b535aaeded5de5120c),
   in whatever half-built state we've left it.
2. **An FDM-printable conversion** — same mechanism, redesigned for
   a desktop printer, ending as a 3MF bundle anyone can print.

What we're intentionally not doing: machining one. Adam already did,
and better than we would — start with
[part 1 of his series ↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SiL8IzJSnyU)
(the rest are on the [sources]({{ 'sources/' | relative_url }}) page).
The vault is his design; this study is our homework on it.

If you'd rather poke the mechanism than read about it, the
[interactive demo]({{ 'interactive/' | relative_url }}) is the place
to start.
